Storm mode: ready

Prepare your gutters for heavy St. Louis storms.

A short check before the rain arrives can reveal blockages, loose sections, and poor discharge points while there is still time to address them.

August 18, 20265 minute readElevated Eddie

Heavy rain puts the entire drainage path under pressure: roof surface, gutter trough, outlet, downspout, elbow, extension, and the ground where water finally exits. A problem anywhere in that chain can push water over an edge or back toward the house.

Use this checklist when a strong storm is in the forecast, after a major leaf drop, or whenever you notice that one area of the house stays wetter than the rest.

1. Start from the ground

Walk the perimeter in daylight and look up at the gutter line. Watch for sections that sag, pull away, tilt outward, or end unevenly. Look for water stains beneath corners and joints. You can spot many problems without climbing.

If you see a fallen branch, visible roof damage, or a gutter hanging loose, keep the area below clear and call for help rather than trying to manage it during bad weather.

2. Clear visible debris safely

Leaves gathered over an outlet can turn a working gutter into an overflowing trough. Remove accessible debris only when conditions are dry and you can do so safely. Never climb during rain, high wind, lightning, or when the ground is unstable. A professional cleaning is the better choice for steep roofs, tall sections, or any setup that makes you uncomfortable.

3. Check every downspout opening

The downspout is the narrowest part of the system and a common place for debris to lodge. Look at the top outlet and bottom elbow where possible. A downspout that sounds full, drains slowly, or releases little water while its gutter overflows may be blocked.

Follow the waterDo not stop at the bottom of the downspout. Make sure the final discharge point sends water away from the foundation and does not create a trip hazard or erosion channel.

4. Secure extensions and splash blocks

Extensions can get bumped by lawn equipment or shifted during yard work. Reposition any section that points back toward the house, has disconnected, or empties into a low spot beside the foundation. The right distance and direction depend on the slope and drainage pattern around the property.

5. Watch one ordinary rain

A moderate rain is an excellent test. From a safe location on the ground, check whether water flows toward the outlets, whether any section spills over, and whether downspouts release steadily. Do not stand beneath questionable gutters or investigate during lightning.

Notice where overflow begins. A single overflowing corner suggests a different issue than an entire long run spilling at once. A short video from the ground can also help explain the problem when you request service.

6. Check the ground after the rain

Once conditions are safe, look for fresh channels in mulch, soil splashed onto siding, puddles near walls, or downspouts that have separated. These clues show where water went—not just where it was supposed to go.

7. Put recurring problems on the list

Cleaning can solve a debris clog. It cannot correct a gutter with poor pitch, too few outlets, inadequate downspout capacity, or loose attachment. If the same spot overflows every storm, the system may need an adjustment or a better drainage plan.
